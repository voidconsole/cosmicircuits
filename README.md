It's 3am.

I still didn't prepare for my speech tomorrow.

I still haven't visited the venue for the 7th time to make sure everything is set up properly.

I have to catch the first bus at 6am tomorrow (technically today) so I can reach 30min before the event.

And yet there I sat, trying to fix the last bug to finish the new project I started 2 hours ago: a hassle-free communication server I thought would be useful for the event.

It's 14th of April.

After months of running around the city for venues, sponsors and logistics, it's finally here.

Cosmic Circuits.
A room full of builders, hardware and ideas.
And currently, a room full of problems I hadn't discovered yet.

Suddenly, the things that only existed on my laptop screen became physical objects.
The banners were up.
The presentation slides were stretching across a massive display at Devfolio HQ.
The same event that had existed only in Discord messages and spreadsheets suddenly had 50 people standing inside it.

My ID card said *Mission Control*.
And apparently being in charge means everyone starts calling you "Sir" all day. Still can't process that one, lol.


We handed everyone stickers. Name. Favourite character. Stick it somewhere visible.
Five minutes later, complete strangers were discussing anime, movies and video games as if they had known each other for years.

We kicked off with quick intros and snacks.

Then we split into two tracks.
Laya, Ryaan, and Trinabh took over Track A to guide the beginners through the basics of CAD and electronics. 
Meanwhile, I ran Track B, pushing the advanced builders straight into IoT, wireless apps, and advanced microcontrollers.

Hidden beneath every seat was a complete electronics kit. ESP32s, motors, raw electronics.

And that communication server I was desperately trying to patch at 3 AM?
It ended up becoming the backbone of the entire event. Every announcement, every attendance form, every resource link, even the WiFi password; the entire operation stood on top of a piece of software that didn't exist 12 hours ago.

Standard engineering protocol, I'd say.

Lunch was McDonald's on the rooftop.
Around this point groups started forming naturally. People were exchanging ideas, discussing projects, and planning things they wanted to build long after Cosmic Circuits was over.

Then we brought in @zikiguy to talk to the crowd, reminding everyone why they fell in love with building things in the first place.

And then, the 3-hour rapid buildathon.
We gave them zero instructions. Just a prompt to create something that mattered, with the people they bonded with, and a room full of components.
To keep things interesting while they were sweating over bugs, we decided to absolutely annoy them over the speakers. We played the exact music tracks they had filled out in their registration forms that morning.
The confusion in the room was immediate, and the realization, beautiful.

By hour three, the sheer caliber of stuff sitting on those tables was insane.
Braille encoded through Morse.
Long-range communication systems.
Adaptive lighting environments.
Teams hacking together workarounds for components they lacked.
Just utterly stunning.

We went through presentations. Asked the tough questions. Argued over judging scores. 
Then gave away the well deserved-prizes: earbuds, soldering irons, gift cards, and the custom stickers and PCB souvenirs we designed.

7 PM hit and passed.
Nobody seemed particularly interested in ending the event.
A problem I was happy to have.
Parents were actively waiting outside, but the hacking just kept going.
Eventually, the room had to clear out.

The team sat down in an empty, quiet Devfolio HQ. We cleaned the place up so they could get back to normal operations the next morning.
We donated the leftover food.

And finally, we returned home for the one thing we hadn't seen in days: sleep, at last.

It feels a bit unsettling now that it's over. Months of frantic logistics and sleepless nights, all leading up to a single, intense peak.
Now the banners are packed away.
The hall is empty.
The server is still running.
And Cosmic Circuits has gone back to being a folder on my computer.
Just with a lot more memories inside it.

Of course, none of this would've happened without a lot of people believing in a crazy idea.
A massive thanks to Hack Club and AMD for funding Cosmic Circuits and making it possible for every participant to attend completely free of charge.
To Devfolio, for opening up their HQ to a bunch of teenagers armed with electronics, soldering irons and questionable levels of confidence. Thank you for the venue, support, and for trusting us enough to let us turn the place into a hardware lab for a day.

And a special thanks to Zikiguy for taking the time to come and speak to everyone.
Cosmic Circuits was about building things, and it was great having someone who genuinely embodies that spirit share his experiences and inspire the room.

And to the team.
Trinabh, who finished the website I started, handled the spreadsheets, and spent the day teaching.
Laya, who designed the custom PCB souvenirs we handed out, travelled across the city with me while hunting for venues, and helped shape the event from the ground up.
Ryaan, who taught workshops, mentored participants, and somehow survived the chaos.
Shreyash and Ryaan, who handled logistics, food, banners, and the countless invisible tasks that somehow determine whether an event succeeds or fails.
Every one of them spent months helping turn an idea into something real.

And judging by the smiles, projects, conversations, and the fact that nobody wanted to leave,
I'd say it was worth it.
